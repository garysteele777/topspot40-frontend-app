The Story of Bb King

Type: feature • Duration: 8.1 minutes

Artist Story MP3
Story Text
B.B. King never just played the blues—he seemed to carry on a conversation with them, one note at a time. You’d watch him on stage, swaying gently side to side, eyes half-closed, and it was clear the guitar wasn’t just an instrument. It was a partner. And nothing tells you more about who he was than the night that partner got her name. It was the winter of 1949 in a little place called Twist, Arkansas. The dance hall was packed, the music was loud, and two men got into it over a woman. In the scuffle they knocked over a barrel of kerosene that was being used to heat the room. Flames raced across the floor. Everyone stampeded for the exits, including B.B. But halfway across the parking lot he realized he’d left his guitar inside. Without hesitating he ran back into the burning building, dodging flames, coughing through smoke, and came out with the instrument tucked under his arm like a rescued child. The next morning he learned the fight had been about a woman named Lucille. From that day forward every guitar he ever owned would bear her name—a private joke, a cautionary tale, and a love story all at once. “Lucille taught me a lesson,” he’d say with that rich chuckle of his. “Don’t run into burning buildings, and don’t fight over women.” That mix of warmth, humor, and hard-headed honesty ran through everything he touched. He was born Riley King on a Mississippi cotton plantation in 1925, lost his mother when he was nine, and was essentially raised by his grandmother. The church gave him his first taste of singing, but the blues found him in the fields. He’d chop cotton all day under that merciless Delta sun, then sit on the porch at night trying to make an old pawn-shop guitar sound like the records he heard on the radio. T-Bone Walker’s electric guitar hit him like a revelation—the way one note could cut right through you. He practiced until his fingers bled, copying every lick he could catch. By the late forties he had made his way to Memphis, sleeping on a friend’s floor and working days at a cotton gin while haunting Beale Street at night. The WDIA radio station gave him his first real break. They let the young man with the big voice spin records and soon let him perform live on air. The program director started calling him the Beale Street Blues Boy. The name got shortened to B.B., and it stuck. From that little radio studio his sound began to travel across the South, reaching people who recognized their own lives in the stories he sang. What always amazed me was how much road he put behind him. For decades he played three hundred-plus dates a year. Not festival sets with light shows—real one-nighters in juke joints, VFW halls, and small theaters where the dressing room might be a broom closet. His big custom bus, which he affectionately called home, rolled through every kind of weather and every kind of town. He used to say the audience deserved the best he had whether there were fifty people or five thousand. That work ethic came from the cotton fields and never left him. One night in 1964 at Chicago’s Regal Theater, that dedication paid off in a moment he would talk about for the rest of his life. The crowd was already on fire before he played a single chord. When he hit the opening notes of “Every Day I Have the Blues,” the place exploded. You can still hear it on the recording—women screaming, men shouting encouragement like they were at a Baptist revival. B.B. stood there letting Lucille cry, then answered her with his own voice, and the conversation between artist and audience became something almost sacred. That live album became a cornerstone for a generation of young musicians who were about to change rock and roll. A few years later came another turning point that still gives me chills. The Fillmore West in San Francisco, 1967. Bill Graham had booked B.B. for what was then a mostly white, mostly young rock audience. B.B. was nervous. These kids didn’t grow up on his music. What if they didn’t get it? He walked out, looked at the sea of long-haired hippies, and then something beautiful happened. They rose to their feet before he even struck a note. When he finally played, the response was so overwhelming he had to stop between songs to wipe his eyes. In that moment the bridge was crossed. The boy from the Mississippi Delta had brought the blues to the children of the Summer of Love, and they recognized truth when they heard it. Eric Clapton, Stevie Ray Vaughan, and a hundred others would later say the same thing in different ways: we all traced our sound back to B.B. He carried himself with a dignity that never felt forced. After shows he would stand in the lobby signing autographs until the last person left. He remembered names. He asked about your family. There’s a famous story from the road where a young promoter tried to slip him an extra hundred dollars under the table. B.B. politely refused it, saying, “Son, if you can’t pay me what we agreed on, just tell me. But don’t ever try to sneak money to me like I’m some kind of hustler.” That same man would turn around and quietly pay medical bills for members of his band or help a struggling blues musician get new tires for his car. The King took care of his own. Even in his later years, when diabetes tried to slow him down, he kept moving. He’d sit on his bus between shows eating pork rinds—his guilty pleasure—and trading stories with the band. He loved to laugh, loved good barbecue, and loved introducing young players to the audience. When he stepped on stage with U2 in the late eighties and traded verses on “When Love Comes to Town,” you could see the pure joy in his face. The kid from the cotton patch was still discovering new friends through music. And that’s the magic that still works today. When the world feels too loud, too fast, too cold, you can put on B.B. King and everything gets simpler. One guitar, one voice, and a lifetime of feeling poured into every phrase. He showed us that the blues isn’t about feeling sorry for yourself. It’s about taking the hard parts of life—lost love, long roads, smoky rooms, burning buildings—and turning them into something that makes other people feel less alone. That’s why Lucille is still singing. That’s why we still need the King. Because somewhere out there tonight, someone is hearing “The Thrill Is Gone” for the first time and realizing their pain has company. And somewhere else, an old soul is smiling because those familiar notes feel like coming home. B.B. King didn’t just play the blues. He befriended them, wrestled with them, and in the end made them beautiful for all of us. And every time Lucille bends a note just so, you can almost hear him say, with that warm voice and that knowing laugh, “We’re gonna be all right.”
